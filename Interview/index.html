INTRO

Mercury Wang is a junior in Sam Fox School studying time based media art. She is also very interested in media studies, majoring in Film and Media Studies with a minor in Human Computer Interaction.

The Interview Recording

01 / Personal Idea [00:21]

So you're heading into your senior year. I know you're super interested in game design, but what's your main focus right now? Are you leaning more toward UI level design or something else?
Yeah, that's a great question. I'm more interested in how to mediate the game's diegesis with our screen and how to introduce the players better into the game world. So I think that's more on the mechanic side.
Looking back at freshman year versus now, how has the way you look at the screen as a medium actually changed? [00:49]
Well, when I was a freshman, I think my understanding of the screen is that it's part of the gaming diegesis or the game apparatus so that it conceals itself as if it's natural, as if the content that it contains is evident by itself. The screen evades into the background of the game. However, now I think the screen itself is speaking out. It's speaking the game's ideology, and it testifies its own existence and also contributes to the medium specificity of this game.
"It's speaking the game's ideology, and it testifies its own existence and also contributes to the medium specificity of this game."

02 / DIEGETIC UI & IMMERSION [01:39]

What's your favorite game right now and what exactly draws you to it?
Yeah, I think that's a really great question. My favorite game is Return of the Obra Dinn. I think it's very shocking, since it's mind-bending. And well, if we think it back to screen, it has a lot of layouts or interfaces. It has a book that recalls memories. It has an illusional world whenever a clock ticks, then the avatar enters, and then also have a time machine kind of agent. And through these various visual elements, the screens become a mediator for them. The players could enter each interface and go out to the interface. And I think that is very fascinating to see how the world view is unfolded through navigation of the interface.
Because now a lot of games incorporate UI directly into the world, like you can point out an actual map instead of opening the menu. So what do you think is the best part of it, and what is the hardest part of making that work? [02:55]
Yeah, I get what you mean. I think the main point is how to integrate the menu part, or to integrate the map or the other relevant icons into the world view of this game. So back to Return of the Obra Dinn, it's interesting, because even when we are navigating the menu, which is like taking a step back from the game world, right? We are still in the game content. We are still justified by the narration. And I think for a lot of, you know, low tech RPGs or low tech FPS that those boys, you know, play, these games have a really like a barrier between menus that are directly addressing the players or the users and the game world. And I think it will be interesting to have either a narration or the camera angles or the UI, UX layout that bridges this gap.

03 / CAMERA & VISUAL CRITIQUE [06:11]

Things are so dynamic compared to flat posters or websites, especially with those 2D styles. How do you feel the camera angles mess with a player's psychology and overall vibe of the screen, since you also learn film studies?
I think compared to film studies, game studies see camera, or they use camera, not only through camera angles or camera movements and cinematography, but they also have to concern the affordances of the camera. Legibility is a priority. Not like films, we can have those visceral interactions or whatever. But for games, a camera has to at least have the function to reveal what's happening, and it has a barrier between pure interaction and pure animation. Because I play very little first-person games, I prefer those puzzle solving games, but I still feel like camera angles is interesting when we think about who initiates the movement. For example, in Chants of Sennaar, sometimes it's the movement of the main character that triggers the 3D camera to chase after it. But sometimes it's maybe a more omnipotent other that initiates the camera and that shift of perspective is what I found interesting.

That's a good one, because legibility is the priority. I think for me, the games visual design I dislike the most is some Japanese and Chinese anime games, for example, Genshin Impact. I don't think that's a proper gender representation.
